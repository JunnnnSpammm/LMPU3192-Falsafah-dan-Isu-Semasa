## 1.0 Introduction
Corruption is defined as an inducement to wrong by improper or unlawful means especially by powerful people (Merriam-Webster, n.d.), and has come to be across all kingdoms and continents independently from one another ever since the dawn of hierarchical society.

In a society driven by incentives such as money or power, it is the most natural progression to occur as the rich and powerful realise that they are able to sway things that are usually out of a normal citizens’ control such as legal judgements in their favour.

Corruption is of course not limited to just the justice system but can occur anywhere in society such as politics, and economics and has adverse effects on each sector (Transparency International, n.d.).

Political corruption occurs when the wealthy have the ability to sway the electoral process. One example of this was during the recent New York Mayoral Election when the wealthy donated millions into Super PACs related to republican candidate Andrew Cuomo to try to damage the reputation of democratic candidate Zohran Mamdani as Mamdani was projected to tax the rich and has an unfavourable view of the ultra wealthy in general (Schneid, 2025).

Economic corruption meanwhile occurs when the wealthy have the ability to sway government officials to make decisions that benefit them in a monetary manner, usually through tax cuts. The most obvious and recent example of this type of corruption is when Donald Trump gave the ultra wealthy massive tax cuts while removing struggling families from government medical insurance and food assistance programmes through the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Drucker, 2025).

Corruption is historically done through the exchange of gifts or currency between the wealthy and an official in power behind closed doors. This practice is so widespread in modern day society that people in power such as CEOs of large companies treat fines for doing unjust to society as the cost of doing business.

With the rise of the middle class in many countries, corruption has become more accessible to the everyday man and is also no longer limited to just the ultra rich and wealthy. In Malaysia for example, it is common knowledge that traffic summons can usually be avoided by bribing police officers, with the practice being known as giving ‘duit kopi’.

## 3.0 Explanation on the Chosen Current Issue
When we talk about corruption, we tend to think of broken laws or greedy individuals. Underneath the headlines however, philosophers like Plato and Kant offer a different perspective on this social issue. Plato urges us to think about the core of our society; he asks how we can structure our leaders and institutions so that power is used for the greater good, and not for selfish ambition. On the other hand, Kant asks us to turn and examine our own personal integrity and ask whether our daily choices could withstand universal moral law. Together they reveal to us that corruption is not just a political failure but a profound crisis of public justice and private morality.

### 3.1 Plato's Perspective on Corruption
Firstly, we will look at Plato’s philosophy in depth. Plato was an ancient Greek philosopher who laid the groundwork for political theory. In his seminal work, “The Republic”, he researches crucial issues facing society such as justice, leadership, education and human nature. It made him a leading figure on the question of corruption. For instance, Plato traces the roots of political and moral decay to imbalances of justice, poor leadership, and unrestrained human desires. In fact, he argues that democracy introduces significant risks of the public electing demagogues, potential dictators, anarchists, and tyrants instead of competent leaders, so called Philosopher Kings (Korab-Karpowicz, n.d.).

#### 3.1.1 Justice and the Three Parts of the Soul
According to Plato, true justice within an individual is achieved when each part of society and each part of the individual performs its proper role, with reason controlling desires. Plato describes the human soul as having three parts: reason, spirit and appetite (Brown, 2017). 

Reason is responsible for our thinking, wisdom and rational decision-making. Reason guides a person when he knows accepting a bribe is wrong, so he should refuse it. Spirit is associated with courage, honour, pride and determination. For example, spirit is guiding us when we stand by our principles even when it is difficult. Lastly, appetite represents our desires for the outside world. It can be anything that tempts us, and most commonly shows up as money, food, pleasure, wealth and power. An example of appetite controlling us would be thinking “RM100,000 is a lot of money. I want it.” Plato raises concerns that the problem occurs when our appetite leads us instead of reason. When this happens, a person may make unjust decisions.

#### 3.1.2 Plato's View on Corruption
After learning about Plato's views regarding justice, how does this relate to corruption? Imagine that a government official is offered a RM50,000 bribe. His appetite would tell him to accept the bribe and be richer, but his reason would remind him not to misuse his position. If his appetite wins and he accepts the bribe, Plato would see this not as simply him breaking the law. Plato saw the deeper problem: his desires have overcome reason and justice. Therefore, corruption can be understood as a form of moral disorder, where personal desires interfere with rational and just behaviour.

#### 3.1.3 The Philosopher-King and Good Leadership
In "The Republic", Plato argues that Philosopher-Kings should rule society . Many people reading this line would presume that Plato was in favour of letting a philosopher become a king. However, that is not the case here; Plato believes that people who possess genuine wisdom, knowledge, justice and rationality should govern society.

It is not hard to see why Plato would have that viewpoint. Any ordinary leader may be motivated by outside temptation, such as money, power, fame or personal interests. Whereas Plato’s ideal Philosopher-King should pursue wisdom, truth, justice and the common good for his people. Failure to do so would lead to economic decline, factionalism, and civil war (Korab-Karpowicz, n.d.).

Plato's view of the Philosopher-King creates a strong connection to corruption. Bad leaders who let their personal desire control them would have higher chances of accepting bribes and allowing corruption. However, a Philosopher-King should govern for the common good. He will let reason, wisdom and justice guide him in governing the society (Brown, 2017). This is the reason why he is less likely to be corrupted. Therefore, you can argue that Plato believes that leaders should be guided by wisdom and justice rather than personal desires for wealth and power. This is useful when discussing corruption in leadership.

#### 3.1.4 Education as a Way to Prevent Corruption
It is also important to understand that Plato does not believe that anyone intelligent can become a good leader. He places great importance on education within society. He believes that education should teach us how to develop wisdom, reason, self-control, justice and moral character. Therefore, when we apply this idea to corruption, education should develop morally responsible and rational leaders. In other words, preventing corruption is not only about creating laws and punishments. Society should also develop people who have the wisdom, self-control, and moral character to resist corruption.

### 3.2 Kant's Perspective on Corruption
After looking at Plato’s perspective on corruption, we can now look at Immanuel Kant’s philosophy. Kant was a German philosopher who focused heavily on ethics and morality. Unlike Plato, who focuses more on justice, leadership and how society should be organised, Kant focuses more on the moral responsibility of an individual. His ethical theory is known as deontological ethics, which is mainly based on the idea of moral duty (Johnson & Cureton, 2004).

#### 3.2.1 Deontological Ethics and Moral Duty
Deontological ethics means that an action is morally right when it follows a moral duty or principle. In simple words, Kant believes that people should do what is right because it is their duty, rather than doing something only because they can gain from it. For example, if a government official is offered a RM50,000 bribe and knows that nobody will find out, Kant would still consider accepting it morally wrong. The official has a duty to reject the bribe, even if accepting it could benefit him personally. Therefore, corruption is wrong because it goes against a person’s moral duty, not simply because they might get caught.

#### 3.2.2 The Categorical Imperative
Another important idea in Kant’s philosophy is the Categorical Imperative. Kant believes that before doing something, we should ask whether the rule behind our action could reasonably be followed by everyone (Johnson & Cureton, 2004). For example, if someone thinks, “It is okay to accept a bribe as long as nobody finds out,” Kant would ask, “What if everyone followed this rule?” If every government official accepted bribes whenever they thought they could get away with it, corruption would become normal and people would lose trust in public institutions. Therefore, the rule behind accepting the bribe cannot be accepted as a universal rule, making the action morally wrong.

#### 3.2.3 Human Dignity
Kant also places great importance on human dignity. He believes that people should be treated as valuable individuals and should not be used only as a way to achieve personal goals (Johnson & Cureton, 2004). This can be connected to corruption because corrupt officials may use their position and other people for their own benefit. For example, an official may give special treatment to someone because they offer him money. In this situation, the official is using his public position as a tool for personal gain. Therefore, corruption can also be seen as a violation of human dignity because other people are being treated as a means to achieve personal goals.

#### 3.2.4 Kant’s Overall View on Corruption Overall
Overall, Kant’s philosophy shows that corruption is morally wrong because it goes against moral duty and cannot be accepted as a universal principle. A person should reject a bribe even when they know they will not be caught. His idea of human dignity also shows that corruption can involve using other people and public positions for personal benefit. Therefore, Kant sees corruption as more than just breaking the law. It is also a problem of individual morality and personal integrity, where people have a responsibility to do what is right even when they have something to gain from doing wrong.

### 3.3 Difference Between Plato’s and Kant’s Perspective on Corruption
The biggest difference between Plato and Kant is how they look at corruption. Plato mainly focuses on justice, while Kant focuses on moral duty. Plato would ask whether a person or action is just and whether the person is allowing reason and justice to control their desires.

On the other hand, Kant would ask whether the action is morally right according to a person’s duty and whether the principle behind the action could be followed by everyone.

#### 3.3.1 Applying Both Philosophers to the Same Corruption Case
Imagine a government official accepts RM50,000 to unfairly approve an application.

From Plato’s perspective, the official is acting unjustly because he allows his desire for money to control his decision. He is also abusing his position and putting his personal interest above the common good. Therefore, Plato would see the corruption as a failure of justice and self-control.

From Kant’s perspective, the official is also acting wrongly because he has failed to follow his moral duty. Accepting a bribe for personal benefit cannot reasonably become a rule that everyone should follow. The official is also using his public position as a means to achieve his own goals. Therefore, Kant would see the action as a violation of moral duty and human dignity.

Overall, both philosophers reject corruption, but they explain it differently. Plato focuses on justice and the character of the person, while Kant focuses on moral duty and the principle behind the action.

## 4.0 Lessons Learned
Plato reminded us that we must actively resist our temptation to offer or accept bribes for personal gain. He taught us that a good leader is a leader that leads with wisdom, intent, justice and has the desire to bring good to his people over his own personal desires by letting reason, wisdom and justice guide him in his decisions. Plato also emphasized using education to prevent corruption by teaching wisdom, reason, self-control, justice and moral characters.

Kent on the other hand taught us to uphold our moral or work duty over our personal gain. Kent also reminded us to evaluate every decision by asking if it would be reasonable for everyone to make the same decision. Kent also emphasises that everyone is a valuable individual and we must treat them as such, not as a tool to achieve our own personal gain.

## 5.0 Conclusion
In conclusion, analyzing corruption through both Plato and Immanuel Kant proved that corruption is not just a simple violation of the law but an issue of self control and personal ethics in society.

While both philosophers reject corruption in different ways, their perspectives complement each other . Plato provides a solution focused more towards moral education, self control and having wise leaders to guide society. Kant on the other hand emphasises moral duty, fairness to all and human dignity. Together, their insights show that legal penalties alone won’t stop corruption as society must work together to raise ethical leaders and responsible citizens.
