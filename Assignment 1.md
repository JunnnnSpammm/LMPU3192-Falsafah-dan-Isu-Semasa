### INTRODUCTION OF PHILOSOPHERS

#### Aristotle (384 - 322 BC)
Aristotle is widely considered one of the most influential figures in the development of Western philosophy and, perhaps more broadly, philosophy around the world. As an ancient Greek philosopher and scientist, Aristotle developed many philosophical and scientific systems that served as frameworks for numerous ancient Islamic and Christian institutions (Kenny 2026).

* Metaphysics

* Eudemian Ethics
* Politics

* Categories
* Organon

#### Confucius (551 - 479 BC)
Confucius, or “Kong Fuzi” in Chinese, was a prominent Chinese philosopher of the Spring and Autumn period who developed a system of social, political, and ethical ideas aimed at restoring peace and moral values during a time of political instability in the Eastern Zhou Dynasty of ancient China (Csikszentmihalyi, 2024).

#### Their Philosophies Related to Rukun Negara
In particular, Aristotle’s ideas in Politics and Metaphysics provide a strong foundation for examining Malaysia’s national ideology, the Rukun Negara. Similarly, the principles articulated in Confucius’s Analects offer valuable insights into the philosophical foundations and underlying values of the Rukun Negara.

### KEPERCAYAAN KEPADA TUHAN - ARISTOTLE

#### 1st Pillar: Kepercayaan kepada Tuhan (Belief in God)
The Rukun Negara principle of Kepercayaan kepada Tuhan emphasizes the importance of belief in God and recognition of a higher power. This principle can be related to Aristotle’s concept of the **“Unmoved Mover”**, which explains the first and ultimate cause behind the movement of the universe. Aristotle’s philosophical understanding of God is developed through his discussion of causation and the existence of an ultimate first principle (Cohen, 2026).

#### Aristotle’s Unmoved Mover
According to Aristotle, everything that is in motion requires something else to cause or initiate its movement. However, there must ultimately be a first cause that does not itself require another cause. Aristotle refers to this first and eternal principle as the **Unmoved Mover**. In *Metaphysics*, Aristotle identifies this ultimate first principle with God and describes it as eternal and unchanging (Aristotle, 1933).

This concept is closely related to the 1st Pillar of Rukun Negara because the Unmoved Mover represents an ultimate divine principle behind the existence and movement of the universe. Aristotle’s argument suggests that the universe cannot be understood only through physical causes, as there must be an ultimate source or first principle. Therefore, his concept of the Unmoved Mover provides a philosophical perspective that can be connected to *Kepercayaan kepada Tuhan*, as both recognize God as an ultimate foundation for understanding existence.

Aristotle’s philosophy also demonstrates that the idea of God can be considered through philosophical reasoning, rather than only through religious belief. Hence, the concept of the Unmoved Mover provides a philosophical connection to *Kepercayaan kepada Tuhan* by explaining God as the ultimate and independent cause of the universe.

### KESETIAAN KEPADA RAJA DAN NEGARA - CONFUCIUS

#### 2nd Pillar: Kesetiaan Kepada Raja dan Negara (Loyalty to King and Country)
The Rukun Negara principle of Kesetiaan Kepada Raja dan Negara reflects Malaysia’s constitutional monarchy. The Yang di-Pertuan Agong is the Head of State and serves as a symbol of national unity, while loyalty to the King and country is intended to strengthen unity among Malaysians. This principle aligns with the Confucian doctrine of *“Zhong”* (忠), alongside *“Li”* (礼) and *“Xiao”* (孝). These concepts are represented by Chinese characters that appear numerous times in the Analects. *“Zhong”* refers to loyalty, *“Li”* refers to etiquette and proper conduct, while *“Xiao”* refers to filial piety, a concept that emphasizes ethical values and children’s respect for their parents (Ungvarsky, 2021).

#### Analects Book 3 “Ba Yi”
According to the translation by Legge (1893), In 3:19, Confucius said:

> “A prince should employ his minister according to the rules of propriety; ministers should serve their prince with faithfulness.”

Closely related to the 2nd Pillar of Rukun Negara, it highlights loyalty between leaders and citizens. However, loyalty should not be one-sided. Confucius emphasizes that rulers must also act responsibly. Hence, loyalty to the King and country promotes unity and stability when both leaders and citizens fulfil their responsibilities.

#### Analects Book 12 “Yan Yuan”
According to Legge’s (1893) translation, in 12:11, Confucius said:

> “There is government, when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son.”

On the surface, Confucius suggests that government exists only when everyone fulfills their respective roles, just as in a good family, where both the father and son have their own roles and responsibilities, similar to 3:19. This can be interpreted as suggesting that good governance requires leaders to uphold good principles in their respective roles (vbr, 2014), while citizens have the responsibility to remain loyal and abide by the rules of their nation.

### KELUHURAN PERLEMBAGAAN - ARISTOTLE

#### 3rd Pillar: Keluhuran Perlembagaan (Constitutional Supremacy)
The Constitution, which is the highest form of law, stands above every governing body and citizen in Malaysia. No individual or institution, including royalty, Parliament and the judiciary, is above it. In his famous work *Politics III and IV*, Aristotle discusses the difference between governments that are regulated by the rule of law and those that are not. He concludes that the rule of law helps guard against arbitrary and injudicious government action. His discussion is helpful for understanding not only the tension between the ‘rule of law’ and the ‘rule of men’, but also the complex role the rule of law plays in modern society. (Wexler & Irvine, 2006, 1)

Aristotle raises a discussion on the distinction between the rule of law and the rule of man via the question: “Is it better to be governed by the best person or the best laws?” In his answer, Aristotle comes down tentatively in favour of the rule of law. (Wexler & Irvine, 2006, 13) 

According to **Aristotle, Politics, Book III, Chapter 16, 1287a28–33**, translated by Harris Rackham. He argues:

> “It seems that someone who orders the law to rule orders God and the mind alone to rule, while someone who orders people to rule adds a wild animal. For they have desires and the heart twists the rule of even the best men. Wherefore law is mind without appetite.”

This passage shows how strongly this comment favoured the rule of law, highlighting that written law reflects no personal desire, unlike the changing whims of individuals subject to emotion. (Clayton, n.d.) So strongly was this comment in favour of the rule of law over the rule of men that Aristotle went back and denied a common Egyptian medical example used by critics against constitutional supremacy. (Wexler & Irvine, 2006, 16)

Below cites the passage from **Aristotle, Politics, Book III, Chapter 16, 1287a33–41**, translated by Charles David Channel Reeve.

> “The analogy from the arts — that it is totally fouled up to do medicine from a book and preferable to use a person with skill — is false. Doctors do not go against written words out of friendship, but earn their fees by curing sick people. Those who rule a polis arrange a lot of things out of spite and friend- ship. If you suspected your doctor of being loyal to your enemies and trying to kill you for pay, you’d look for your cure in books.

Moreover, in **Aristotle, Politics, Book IV , Chapter 1, 1289a**, translated by Benjamin Jowett. Aristotle writes:

> “A constitution is the organisation of offices in a state, and determines what is to be the governing body, and what is the end of each community. But laws are not to be confounded with the principles of the constitution: they are the rules according to which the magistrates should administer the state, and proceed against offenders.”

This showcased how Aristotle believed that the constitution ruled above the governing body. Acting as the guide on how future laws should be formed to align with the constitution. Not only that, but the constitution should also serve as the compass in guiding the country’s future actions.

### KEDAULATAN UNDANG-UNDANG - ARISTOTLE

#### 4th Pillar: Kedaulatan Undang-Undang (Rule of Law)
The Rukun Negara principle of Kedaulatan Undang-Undang upholds that law governs impartially and equally over every citizen, and that no one, including those in power, may act outside its bounds. While the 3rd Pillar establishes that the constitution stands supreme over all governing bodies, the 4th Pillar concerns a related but distinct idea: that law must rule impartially in place of the personal will of rulers.

According to Aristotle, Politics, Book III, Chapter 11, 1282b1–6, translated by Harris Rackham. He writes:

> “It is proper for the laws when rightly laid down to be sovereign, while the ruler or rulers in office should have supreme powers over matters as to which the laws are quite unable to pronounce with precision because of the difficulty of making a general rule to cover all cases.”

This shows Aristotle placing law above the discretion of individual rulers, permitting personal judgement only where the law cannot reach. This reflects the 4th Pillar, as governance should follow fixed legal standards rather than the preferences of those in office.

Moreover, in Aristotle, Politics, Book III, Chapter 16, 1287b1–5, translated by Harris Rackham. Aristotle argues:

> “It is clear that when men seek for what is just they seek for what is impartial; for the law is that which is impartial.”

This captures the essence of Kedaulatan Undang-Undang: law is valued because it does not favour one person over another, unlike a ruler who may be swayed by friendship or self-interest. Aristotle's identification of justice with impartiality reinforces why Malaysia's legal system treats all citizens equally under the same rules.

### KESOPANAN DAN KESUSILAAN - CONFUCIUS

#### 5th Pillar: Kesopanan dan Kesusilaan (Courtesy and Morality)
The principle of Kesopanan dan Kesusilaan emphasizes the importance of courteous behaviour, morality, respect, and appropriate conduct in society. This principle can be related to Confucian concepts of *“Ren”* (仁) and *“Li”* (礼). *“Ren”* (仁) refer to kindness and consideration for others, while *“Li”* (礼) refers to proper manners, and respect.

#### Analects Book 10 “Heang Tang”
According to the translation by Legge (1893), in 10:2, Confucius said:

> “When the ruler was present, his manner displayed respectful uneasiness; it was grave, but self-possessed.”

This shows that Confucius emphasized behave appropriately and respectfully according to different social situations. This can be interpreted as reflecting the Confucian concept of *“Li”* (礼), which emphasizes proper conduct, ritual propriety, and respectful behaviour towards others (Radice, 2017).

This closely related to the 5th Pillar of Rukun Negara, Kesopanan dan Kesusilaan, because both emphasize respectful and appropriate behaviour. People should control their words and actions and treat others with courtesy.

Confucius also emphasized *“Ren”* (仁), which encourages people to be kind and considerate for others. Therefore, *“Ren”* (仁) provides moral value, while *“Li”* (礼) teach people on how to express that value through proper behaviour. These ideas can help create peaceful relationships and a respectful society.

In conclusion, Confucius's teachings on *“Ren”* (仁) and *“Li”* (礼) are closely related to Kesopanan dan Kesusilaan because both emphasize kindness, respect, good manners, and moral behaviour.
